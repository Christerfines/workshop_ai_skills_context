**Professor Iris Vance:** And that's really all this is — vocabulary. Five terms that every other episode in this series is going to use without stopping to define. Nothing case-specific, nothing about NordicBike. Just the words.

**Mira Chen:** Start with the most basic one: what's a "model call"?

**Marcus Webb:** It's one request-response round trip to an AI model — you send it some text, called the input or the prompt, and it sends back some text, the output. That's it. When people in this workshop say "one call" versus "two calls," they mean exactly that: did you ask the model to do its job in a single round trip, or did you break the work into two separate requests, maybe to two different models, with the first one's output feeding into the second one's input.

**Professor Iris Vance:** Think of it like sending someone a written question and getting a written answer back, versus asking a first person a question and then handing what they told you to a second person to act on. Same basic shape as a phone call or an email, just automated and very fast.

**Mira Chen:** Next word, and it comes up constantly: "token."

**Professor Iris Vance:** A token is roughly a small chunk of text — sometimes a whole word, often a piece of a word, sometimes just punctuation. You don't need to know the exact rule for how text gets split into tokens; what you need is the intuition that more text means more tokens, and models are priced and measured by token count, both for what you send in and what you get back. When this workshop talks about a prompt being "19,800 tokens," that's just a way of saying "this much text, measured in the model's own units," which happens to be a more precise and more cost-relevant measure than counting words or characters.

**Marcus Webb:** And the practical reason it matters: tokens cost money, and more tokens generally means a slower response too. So "reduce tokens" in this workshop always means the same thing — send the model less text — while "don't lose correctness" means: be careful about which text you cut.

**Mira Chen:** Third word — "context," or "context window."

**Professor Iris Vance:** The context is everything you put in front of the model for a given call — the instructions, any source documents, any prior conversation, all of it, bundled into that one request. The context window is the upper limit on how much of that a given model can accept at once. In this workshop, "context" mostly just means "everything in the prompt" — and a recurring theme is that it's very easy to put more into that prompt than the task actually needs.

**Mira Chen:** Fourth — "model tier."

**Marcus Webb:** Different models are not interchangeable — some are faster and cheaper but less capable of complex reasoning, some are slower and more expensive but handle harder judgment calls more reliably. A "tier" is just a label for where a given model sits on that spectrum. This workshop uses three tiers, numbered by capability and cost, and a big part of what it teaches is sending each piece of work to the tier that actually matches how hard that piece of work is, rather than defaulting to the strongest tier for everything out of habit or caution.

**Professor Iris Vance:** You don't need to know anything about specific models to follow along — just the general idea that "tier" means "how capable, and how expensive, is the model doing this particular piece of work."

**Mira Chen:** Last one — "JSON," and "typed payload."

**Professor Iris Vance:** JSON is just a plain-text way of writing structured data — key-value pairs, the way a form has labeled fields instead of a blank paragraph. Instead of writing "the customer's name is Anna and she bought the bike on March 10th" as a sentence, you'd write something like a field called customer name with the value Anna, and a separate field called purchase date with the value March 10th. It's not a programming skill you need to already have — you'll see plenty of concrete examples once the workshop starts. The only thing worth knowing now is the word itself and the basic shape: labeled fields instead of prose.

**Marcus Webb:** And "typed payload" is just JSON used in a specific way — a small, deliberate set of labeled fields being passed from one step of a pipeline to the next, as opposed to just dumping a wall of text across that same handoff. You'll hear "payload, not a dump" a lot in this series. Now you know what both halves of that phrase mean.
